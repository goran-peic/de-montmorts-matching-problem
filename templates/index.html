De Montmort's Matching Problem

Hwang & Blitzstein · Introduction to Probability · Example 1.6.4

§ 01 — The Problem

What is De Montmort's Matching Problem?

Proposed by the French mathematician Pierre Rémond de Montmort in 1708, the matching problem is one of the earliest and most elegant results in classical probability theory.

Imagine a shuffled deck of n cards numbered 1 through n. A second deck, also numbered 1 through n, is laid out in order. Cards from the shuffled deck are turned over one by one alongside the ordered deck. A match occurs whenever the shuffled card and the ordered card share the same number at the same position. The central question is:

"What is the probability that at least one match occurs?"

Equivalently, we ask for the probability that a uniformly random permutation of n elements has at least one fixed point — a position i where the permutation maps i to itself. Despite the intuitive complexity of the question, the answer converges rapidly to a beautifully simple limit as n grows.

§ 02 — The Analytic Solution

Inclusion–Exclusion and the Limit

Let Ai be the event that position i is a match. By the inclusion–exclusion principle, the probability of the union \( P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) \) is:

Inclusion–Exclusion Expansion
\[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k+1} \binom{n}{k} \frac{(n-k)!}{n!} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!} \]

Each term \( P(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_k}) = (n-k)!/n! \) counts the fraction of permutations that fix at least those k specified positions. There are \(\binom{n}{k}\) such intersections, each contributing equally.

Closed Form
\[ P(\text{at least one match}) \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!} \]

Recognising the partial sum of the Taylor expansion \( e^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k / k! \), the probability that no match occurs converges to \( e^{-1} \approx 0.3679 \) as \( n \to \infty \). Consequently, the probability of at least one match converges to:

Limiting Probability
\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(\text{at least one match}) \;=\; 1 - e^{-1} \;\approx\; 0.6321 \]

Remarkably, this limit is approached very quickly — already for \(n \geq 5\) the probability stabilizes to four decimal places. The result is surprising: no matter how large the deck, there is always roughly a 63.2% chance that some card lands in its "correct" position.

§ 03 — Simulation Approach

Estimating via Monte Carlo

While the analytic solution elegantly closes the problem, a Monte Carlo simulation provides complementary intuition: it lets us watch the probability converge rather than derive it symbolically.

For each deck size n from 1 to the chosen maximum, the simulation independently generates a large number of random permutations and records whether any position is a fixed point. The estimated probability is simply the fraction of trials in which at least one match occurred:

\(\hat{p}_n = \text{wins} \;/\; \text{sample size}\)

With a sufficiently large sample, \(\hat{p}_n\) tracks the analytic curve closely and the plateau near \(1 - e^{-1}\) becomes clearly visible. Increasing the number of simulations reduces variance and sharpens the estimate.

§ 06 — The Central Limit Theorem

Why Sample Means Become Normal

The simulation above showed something remarkable: regardless of deck size, the probability of a match stabilizes near \(1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321\). But this raises a natural statistical question — if we did not know this number and had to estimate it from data, what would repeated estimates look like? The answer is provided by one of the most profound theorems in all of mathematics: the Central Limit Theorem.

Central Limit Theorem (informal statement)
\[ \bar{X}_n \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \quad \xrightarrow{\;d\;} \quad \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]

Formally, let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) be independent and identically distributed random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). The CLT states that the distribution of the standardized sample mean

Standardised Form
\[ Z_n \;=\; \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\; \mathcal{N}(0,\,1) \]

converges in distribution to a standard normal, regardless of the shape of the underlying distribution. The Bernoulli trials in our matching game — each a 0 (no match) or 1 (at least one match) — are precisely the kind of i.i.d. sequence to which the CLT applies. The population mean is \(\mu = 1 - e^{-1}\) and the population variance is \(\sigma^2 = \mu(1-\mu) \approx 0.232\).

📐 What CLT tells us about spread

The standard error of the mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). Doubling sample size cuts the standard deviation of the sampling distribution by \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41\), not by 2. This square-root relationship is the famous Inverse Square Law in sampling.

🎯 What CLT tells us about shape

No matter how skewed or irregular the parent distribution, the histogram of many sample means will be bell-shaped. This is why the normal distribution appears so pervasively in empirical science — it is the limiting attractor of averaging processes.

🔬 Confidence intervals

CLT is the engine behind every confidence interval. A 95% CI for a mean is \(\bar{X} \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). Narrowing the interval by half requires four times as many observations — a direct consequence of the \(\sqrt{n}\) denominator.

⚡ Power & sample size

In hypothesis testing, statistical power — the probability of detecting a true effect — depends critically on \(n\). Larger samples compress the sampling distribution, making it easier to distinguish signal from noise. The CLT provides the theoretical scaffolding for all such power calculations.

In the sections that follow, we treat \(1 - e^{-1}\) as an unknown population parameter and watch the CLT in action: three different sample sizes will produce three different sampling distributions, each centred near the true value but with starkly different widths.

§ 08 — CLT Results

Sampling Distributions & the Inverse Square Law

The three curves above are each a kernel density estimate (approximated here by a fitted Gaussian) of 200 independent sample means. Their collective behaviour illustrates three essential principles:

I. The Central Limit Theorem

All three distributions are centred near \(1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321\). Despite the underlying distribution being Bernoulli, repeated averaging produces bell-shaped curves — exactly as the CLT predicts. The true parameter sits at the heart of each bell, confirming that sample means are unbiased estimators of the population mean.

II. The Inverse Square Law

The standard error scales as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Going from 100 to 1,000 games (10× more data) shrinks the spread by a factor of \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\). From 100 to 5,000 (50× more data), the spread shrinks by \(\sqrt{50} \approx 7.07\). This diminishing return means that buying precision becomes increasingly expensive — a cornerstone insight for any empirical study.

III. Power & Precision

A narrow sampling distribution means we can reliably distinguish our estimate from alternative hypotheses. The tall, thin curve for the largest sample corresponds to a high-power test. In practice, this informs every sample-size calculation in medicine, A/B testing, polling, and experimental physics — the CLT guarantees normality; the Inverse Square Law dictates cost.
