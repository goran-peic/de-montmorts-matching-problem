De Montmort's Matching Problem

Hwang & Blitzstein · Introduction to Probability · Example 1.6.4

§ 01 — The Problem

What is De Montmort's Matching Problem?

Proposed by the French mathematician Pierre Rémond de Montmort in 1708, the matching problem is one of the earliest and most elegant results in classical probability theory.

Imagine a shuffled deck of n cards numbered 1 through n. A second deck, also numbered 1 through n, is laid out in order. Cards from the shuffled deck are turned over one by one alongside the ordered deck. A match occurs whenever the shuffled card and the ordered card share the same number at the same position. The central question is:

"What is the probability that at least one match occurs?"

Equivalently, we ask for the probability that a uniformly random permutation of n elements has at least one fixed point — a position i where the permutation maps i to itself. Despite the intuitive complexity of the question, the answer converges rapidly to a beautifully simple limit as n grows.

§ 02 — The Analytic Solution

Inclusion–Exclusion and the Limit

Let Ai be the event that position i is a match. By the inclusion–exclusion principle, the probability of the union \( P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) \) is:

Inclusion–Exclusion Expansion
\[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k+1} \binom{n}{k} \frac{(n-k)!}{n!} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!} \]

Each term \( P(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_k}) = (n-k)!/n! \) counts the fraction of permutations that fix at least those k specified positions. There are \(\binom{n}{k}\) such intersections, each contributing equally.

Closed Form
\[ P(\text{at least one match}) \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!} \]

Recognising the partial sum of the Taylor expansion \( e^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k / k! \), the probability that no match occurs converges to \( e^{-1} \approx 0.3679 \) as \( n \to \infty \). Consequently, the probability of at least one match converges to:

Limiting Probability
\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(\text{at least one match}) \;=\; 1 - e^{-1} \;\approx\; 0.6321 \]

Remarkably, this limit is approached very quickly — already for \(n \geq 5\) the probability stabilises to four decimal places. The result is surprising: no matter how large the deck, there is always roughly a 63.2% chance that some card lands in its "correct" position.

§ 03 — Simulation Approach

Estimating via Monte Carlo

While the analytic solution elegantly closes the problem, a Monte Carlo simulation provides complementary intuition: it lets us watch the probability converge rather than derive it symbolically.

For each deck size n from 1 to the chosen maximum, the simulation independently generates a large number of random permutations and records whether any position is a fixed point. The estimated probability is simply the fraction of trials in which at least one match occurred:

\(\hat{p}_n = \text{wins} \;/\; \text{sample size}\)

With a sufficiently large sample, \(\hat{p}_n\) tracks the analytic curve closely and the plateau near \(1 - e^{-1}\) becomes clearly visible. Increasing the number of simulations reduces variance and sharpens the estimate.
